Summary

State of the investigation. The project asked whether the low-level feedback loop of a torque-controlled manipulator can be learned so that one controller is competitive with, or better than, the best hand-tuned classical law in every operating regime at once. After seventeen reports on a simulated Franka Panda the answer is yes on 28 of the 33 pre-registered family-and-group rows, with the five misses on one family at 5 to 6%. The controller that does this does not replace the classical loop; it adds to it. A 1 kHz PD law with the nominal inverse-dynamics feedforward runs under a network that, every 10 ms, sets bounded per-joint gain multipliers and a bounded torque residual from 100 ms of proprioceptive history and an estimate of the external torque. It is trained in two stages, both in simulation: a residual by backpropagation through the plant on free-space references, then imitation of two privileged teachers, the ideal-robot torque in free space and a contact-impedance law in contact. It tracks free motion at 2.6 mrad against 4.9 for the feedforward PD, holds at 1.6 against 8.1, waits at a blocked surface with 5 N above the 15 N cap where the best classical pushes 23 N, presses inside the 7 to 12 N band, places a block at 5 N against 14, and moves by at most 5% under a 5 ms compute latency.

Of the agenda's hypothesis space, three parameterisations by three learning routes, one cell survived: the residual form, trained by gradients through the plant and finished by imitation of a specified behaviour. Reinforcement learning (five rounds), direct torque networks and gain schedules on their own did not earn a place; a learned dynamics model did, as a training medium, but was not carried to the final plant. The remaining gaps are a smoothness miss on policy-like targets, a press band that sits 5 N above the cap on sliding contact, task-success rates on sixteen cases, privileged labels without a hardware path yet, and every claim resting on three seeds and sixteen cases per family. Sections 6 to 8 list the gaps, what to revisit when the method is scaled, and what a run costs.

1. The problem and the setting

1.1 The question

Every torque-controlled robot closes a feedback loop between the policy's targets and the motors, usually a PD or impedance law at 100 Hz to 1 kHz with gains tuned by hand. The research agenda argues that this loop should be learned: no gain set serves every task, every phase of a task and every robot instance; policies treat the loop as a black box and pay for it in jerky and unstable behaviour; retuning by hand does not scale across robots, tasks and contact modes, and switching gain sets stably is hard in dynamic tasks; and a controller learned from proprioception in simulation has a small sim-to-real gap. The agenda's design space is three parameterisations (learned PD gains, PD plus a residual, direct torque prediction) by three learning routes (a known differentiable model, a learned dynamics model, reinforcement learning). Section 3 maps what was tried in it and what was added to it.

1.2 The plant

MuJoCo at 1 kHz with torque actuators at the datasheet limits. Two plants: v2, the Panda with a 2 cm spherical tool (reports 07 to 13), and v3, the Panda with the Robotiq 2F-85 baked closed into one rigid tool body (reports 14 to 17; opened to 38 mm for pick-and-place), each with a table and a wall under the explicit soft contact model of ADR 0001 (about 60 kN/m) and two compliant surfaces held out for evaluation. Every evaluation and training window draws a robot instance (link masses ±5%, inertias ±10%, centres of mass ±5 mm, joint damping 0.5 to 2×, armature ±20%, dry friction 0 to 0.3 Nm, actuator gain ±3%), a payload of 0 to 2 kg (3 kg in training and in one held-out group) and sensor noise. Every controller, classical or learned, uses the datasheet model only; the simulated plant's parameters are never read.

1.3 The protocol

Protocol v3 (ADR 0009): 16 test cases per family, each with a robot instance, a payload of 0 to 2 kg and sensor noise; a disjoint training split generates the training references and a validation split selects checkpoints. Held-out groups: workspace, speed ×1.5 and ×2, payload 3 kg, doubled instance deviations, compliant surfaces of 2 kN/m and 500 N/m; a 5 ms compute-latency group for the learned controllers. 1.4 Baselines, ceiling and criterion

Classical set. Four fixed controllers, each one gain set for all families: a PD plus gravity law whose gains CMA-ES tuned on the mean tracking composite over the seven families (four training cases each), a second set tuned on the cap composite with the same budget, and the inverse-dynamics feedforward variant of each (the computed-torque term of the reference added to the PD). No controller is tuned per family. 'The best classical controller of the family' is the one of the four with the lowest cap composite on that family and group; it is the reference of every comparison, and this bar is optimistic by construction: the selection is made per row on the test cases, whereas each learned controller is one network selected once on the validation split. Section 2.3 also reports the comparison against a single classical controller.

Ceiling. The privileged contact-impedance oracle around the PD (ADR 0008): it knows the surface and the payload and executes the wanted contact law exactly. It marks what the contact families can give; it is never a baseline.

Criterion (ADR 0010, pre-registered before Milestone 10): within 5% of the best classical or better on every family and held-out group composite (33 rows), better on the three contact families and both task-success rates, three seeds with paired intervals excluding zero where a win is claimed, and no divergence under a 5 ms compute latency.

2. The controller that resulted

2.1 The law

At 1 kHz the commanded torque is τ = α ∘ (K_p e + K_d ė) + M(q) q̈_ref + C(q, q̇_ref) q̇_ref + g(q) + Δτ with e = q_ref − q, the shared gains K_p, K_d of the tracking-tuned PD, the nominal model's M, C and g, and q̈_ref the clipped central difference of q̇_ref (held targets contribute no feedforward). The per-joint multipliers α (stiffness in [0.02, 1.5], damping in [0.3, 2] of the shared gains) and the residual Δτ (bounded at half the torque limit) are the network's outputs, held for 10 ms between decisions; a 5 ms compute latency is modelled, so a decision observed at t acts from t + 5 ms. The network is a two-layer MLP of width 128 on the current reference and state and a two-scale history, 100 ms at 10 ms spacing plus the last 32 ms at 1 ms, of q, q̇, the commanded torque and the external-torque estimate (the measured link-side torque minus the nominal model's prediction). Every input is a signal the Panda exposes.

2.2 Training

Stage 0, gains. Shared gains by CMA-ES on the tracking composite of four training cases per family (40 iterations).

Stage 1, the base residual. A torque residual on the feedforward PD, trained by a short-horizon actor-critic through the plant: windows of 2 s with a random instance, payload and (in 30% of the hold and free windows) a 10 N pulse; a reward of bounded tracking kernels (20 mrad half-width) with a light torque-rate penalty (weight 0.003); a privileged critic (payload, contact force and a preview of the reference as inputs; no torque history); gradients by MuJoCo's finite-difference transition derivatives, clipped per sample at three times the batch median. The base trains on the free-space families only (hold, free motion, policy-like, step), 250 iterations of 24 windows, and its checkpoint is selected on the free-space cap composite of four validation cases per family (ADR 0010, amendment 2).

Stage 2, the student. The base converted to the gain-multiplier form (head at neutral, exact warm start) with the external-torque input added, trained by DAgger: the teacher drives in the first iteration only, then the student drives and the teachers label every decision. In free space the label is the ideal-robot torque: the torque the sampled plant needs so that the nominal robot's response to the nominal law results, external loads included and referenced to the nominal law (the PD plus feedforward without the student's own residual); decisions where the nominal law saturates or the correction exceeds 30 Nm are dropped. In contact the label is the oracle's torque: the base's normal force replaced by a force-bounded spring on the actual error, 7 N + 500 N/m × e capped at 15 N, the first-contact point held when the reference is blocked, a 200 ms blend back on release, the payload's weight compensated. Free-space decisions enter the label loss with weight 3. Twelve iterations of 48 episodes (270 to 300 thousand labelled decisions); the checkpoint with the best validation cap composite (two cases per free-space family, eight per contact family) is kept. Three seeds of everything.

The student meets 28 of 33 rows. It wins the three contact families by 13 to 60% (blocked targets at 0.40 to 0.51 of the best classical in every group), every free-space family except one (hold 0.47, free motion 0.36 to 0.90, steps 0.79), both compliant surfaces, the 3 kg payload group, and push success (52 against 31%, eight or nine of sixteen boxes against five); placement is at the ceiling for everyone (100%) with the student placing at a third of the force. The five misses are the policy-like family (held targets at 10 to 50 Hz) at 1.05 to 1.06 in every group: lower tracking error than the PD, higher torque rate. The same student distilled from the oracle alone meets 29 rows and misses free motion where the nominal model is accurate (6.9 against 4.9 mrad); the Milestone 10 student, without the model term in its base, met 27. No learned controller diverges anywhere, and the residual students move by at most 5% at 5 ms of compute latency. The free-motion win is a mean over cases, not a uniform one: per seed the student loses 4 to 6 of the 16 cases, all where the nominal model is accurate and the feedforward PD is already at 1 to 3 mrad, by up to a factor of 4.8 (5.3 against 1.1 mrad, a 47 mrad burst on one joint); it wins the rest, by the most where the model is wrong (18 to 2.4 mrad on the PD's worst case).

Against one classical controller for everything, the tracking-tuned PD with feedforward, the student meets 28 of 33 rows with the same five policy-like misses, because on that family the feedforward PD is the per-family best anyway; against the cap-tuned PD alone it meets all 33. The criterion of ADR 0010 is the harder one, against the per-family selection, and the grid above reports that.

Beyond the parameterisation-by-route matrix, the deep dives explored four further axes.

  • Inputs. A proprioceptive history is the implicit system identification the agenda asked for and was kept throughout (100 ms at 10 ms spacing, later with 32 ms dense). The external-torque estimate from the robot model was added in D5 and is the contact cue (D6). A target preview (future targets in the observation) added nothing beyond the classical feedforward and was shelved; a target history halved the policy-like torque rate in D3 and was not carried; Fourier features and an output low-pass were planned as a representation study and not run; a tool-frame wrench was shelved for its sim-to-real gap.
  • Rewards. Instantaneous force penalties (D2) destabilised training and were an optimisation failure as weighted; filtered force terms (D5) still cost free-space tracking unless detached from the gradient; the cap reward with contact-maintenance terms ranked a compliant exemplar first but no optimiser found that behaviour; the final recipe uses the reward only for free space and specifies contact as a law.
  • Protocol and plant. Protocol v1 (nominal robot, six regimes), v2 (instances, payloads, blocked targets, the 15 N cap, stop-and-wait, release metrics), v3 (gripper, press band 7 to 12 N, link clearance, task families). Three harness defects were found by probes and corrected with errata: blocked references without a block (60% of them), link collisions on the wall branch, and an evaluation worker that ignored the case's perturbation.
  • Classical additions. The inverse-dynamics feedforward of the reference halves the tracking-tuned PD's free-motion error (8.8 to 4.9 mrad) and was, until the two-teacher student, worth more than any learned part on that family; phase-scheduled gains (M6) cut impact peaks at the price of torque spikes and failed with a payload.

4. What worked, and why

  1. A proprioceptive history identifies the payload. From the first 1 kHz residual on (D2), learned controllers held a loaded arm at 3.4 to 5 mrad where the soft PD sagged to 16.7; on the 3 kg held-out payload the handoff controller tracks free motion at 5.0 against 20.6 mrad. The mechanism is implicit identification from the history, as the agenda anticipated.
  2. Gradients through the plant, if tamed. Backpropagation through MuJoCo's finite-difference derivatives works once the chained Jacobians through contact are clipped per sample at a multiple of the batch median, the horizon is short with a critic bootstrap (gamma 0.995, ramped in), the tracking kernels are bounded (20 mrad) and the torque-rate penalty is light (0.003; at 0.03 it dominated the objective and the residual gained by softening). With those four elements the residual beats the tuned PD on every family and group of protocol v2 (D3) and is the base of every later student.
  3. Specify the contact behaviour, then imitate it. The wanted response was written as a law, a force-bounded spring on the actual error with stop-and-wait when blocked, executed by a privileged oracle, validated against the metrics, and distilled by DAgger in ten minutes per seed: blocked excess force 57 to 15 N on the sphere plant, and on the gripper 5 N against 23 for the best classical and 43 for the student's own base, apparent stiffness 1440 to 390 N/m, and a contact stiffness near the teacher's 500 N/m on held-out compliant surfaces where the PD keeps its own 1 kN/m. Five rounds of reinforcement learning never found this behaviour by exploration and did not improve it once found.
  4. The external torque is the cue. The link-side torque minus the model's prediction detects contact within 50 ms for 93 to 97% of onsets (error and velocity cues: 61 to 78%), and separates contact from a payload once payload-carrying free motion is in the fit set. Without it the features of the residual carried contact only through the accumulated error, 0.7 s late.
  5. Protect the base during distillation. With the teacher driving only once and free-space decisions weighted three times, the student keeps its base's free space within 3%; the first recipe (the teacher driving in three iterations, unweighted labels) gave up 20 to 40% of it.
  6. Give the learned controller the model term. The nominal inverse-dynamics feedforward in the PD base, a free-space-only base and selection on free space took the student from 11.9 to 6.9 mrad; the student reproduces its base, so what remained was the base's reward.
  7. Ideal-robot labels as the free-space teacher, referenced to the nominal law. Supervised labels that make the sampled plant reproduce the nominal robot's response halve the feedforward PD's error (2.6 mrad) and hold at 1.6 against 8.1, in every seed, provided the label is taken relative to the nominal law rather than the student's own command (item 6 of Section 5).
  8. Keep the 1 kHz loop under the learned part. The residual form rides on the PD between decisions and under latency: the students move by at most 5% at 5 ms. The same history, same training, same teachers with a direct torque output lose every free-space family (Section 5, item 2).
  9. Contact behaviour transfers across plants. The student trained on the sphere plant ran unchanged on the gripper plant and kept its contact behaviour (2.3 N above the cap on blocked targets); its free-space edge did not transfer and was restored by retraining.

5. What did not work, and why

Each item is an investigated negative result unless marked budget-limited; the mechanism is stated where it was established from saved episodes, reward decompositions or probes.

  1. Training through the penalty-contact torch model (M2). Validation in the plant worsened whenever the contact stage entered training while the in-model loss fell: exploitation of the contact approximation. Superseded by the plant as the medium; free-space training through the model was fine, and a learned model matched it (M4).
  2. Direct torque networks. The worst parameterisation through the known model (M2), untrainable by PPO (M5), 136 to 141 N blocked excess at 1 kHz (D2), collapsed at the contact stage under finite-difference gradients (D3), and in round 2 the best hold tracker as a base yet the worst student: its torque is open-loop for the 10 ms it is held, it chatters at 1953 Nm/s, and a 5 ms latency degrades it threefold. One architecture and recipe were tried; the result is about this recipe.
  3. Reinforcement learning, five rounds. PPO was worse than backpropagation for every parameterisation under protocol v1 (M5); in the plant it reached parity with its warm start and cut forces by a flinch at the reward's soft limit (D4); under the cap reward it pushed less but stayed stiff and never explored the gain head (D5); it neither improved the distilled students nor produced stop-and-wait from the D5 arm (D6). The training return rose in every round while the protocol's contact composites did not follow. The diagnosis from saved episodes: yielding at a blocked surface is a large, coordinated residual torque that no optimiser found by exploration, and on sliding references the reward preferred intermittent light contact. Budget per round was small (150 to 300 updates); the pattern was the same in each.
  4. Force penalties as reward terms. Instantaneous terms made the contact-stage gradients three to four times larger and noisier and trained worse than the no-force twins (D2); filtered terms attached to the gradient cost free-space tracking three times in a row (D5). Detached from the gradient they were harmless and taught the features to sense contact, but they did not produce compliance.
  5. Selection on the cap composite for a base. The actor-critic residual's best checkpoint under the cap composite was a contact-stage iteration whose free space was half as good as its stage-1 optimum (M10). A selection rule changes what a method is; the base is now selected on free space and the contact behaviour is the student's.
  6. Ideal-robot labels under the student's own command. Labelling the torque the plant needs under the student's full command lets the target drift with the student's residual: every DAgger iteration degraded hold and free motion (M10's ideal-robot run; the first two-teacher form). Referenced to the nominal law the same labels are the best free-space teacher.
  7. Learned gains without a residual, phase-scheduled gains, the target preview. Gain schedules alone went soft everywhere (M2); hand-scheduled gains spike torque at switches and sag under a payload (M6); a preview of future targets adds nothing beyond the classical feedforward (D6). The bounded gain multipliers survive only as a head next to the residual.
  8. Small validation sets. Two cases per family misranked the contact arms of D4 against the 16-case test set; eight cases per contact family (two to four per free-space family) are used since, and no ranking is claimed from training-time validation.

6. Where the gaps are

  1. Policy-like targets, the one miss. At 1.05 to 1.06 of the best classical in every group, with better tracking and a higher torque rate. The ideal-robot labels are dropped where the nominal law saturates, which is exactly at target jumps, so the student's behaviour there is the base's; nothing in the labels asks for smoothness.
  2. The press band against the cap. The 7 N floor puts pressing inside the 7 to 12 N band (10.6 N mean) at 5 N above the 15 N cap on the sliding families, where light contact at 9 to 15 N detaches 20 to 70 times per slide for every controller on both plants (report 14). The contact-loss metric on sliding families measures the simulator as much as the controller; the plant question comes before further tuning of the teacher.
  3. Task success on sixteen cases. Push success 52% against 31% is eight or nine boxes against five; box yaw is the failure for every controller and is a task-policy problem. Placement is at the ceiling for everyone, so the task half of the criterion is met on one task and tied on the other.
  4. Privileged supervision. Both teachers use what only a simulator gives: the oracle knows the surface and the payload; the ideal-robot label is the sampled plant's inverse dynamics. On hardware the oracle's role could be taken by a force-sensed or estimated impedance law, and the ideal-robot label needs a system identification or a learned plant model (Milestone 4's model is the candidate). Neither path has been built.
  5. Contact sensing stays weak in the features. Linear probes decode contact from the trunk features at 0.71 to 0.76 balanced accuracy for every controller, students included, although the behaviour is right; the external-torque input carries the cue, the features do not represent it explicitly.
  6. The classical bar is modest. Forty CMA-ES iterations on four cases per family, one tuning seed, a feedforward from the nominal model without instance adaptation. A better-tuned classical set, or an adaptive classical law, would raise the bar the learned controller is measured against.
  7. The ceiling is the teacher. The student reaches the oracle's contact composites; whether a better contact law exists is not tested, since the oracle's parameters (500 N/m, 0.7 damping ratio, 200 ms blend) were set once from hardware practice.
  8. Smoothness and error bursts. The student's torque rate in free motion is above the stiff PD's (159 against 115 Nm/s) and higher in contact; it is within the protocol's weight but not a win. In free motion the student also shows transient error bursts on single joints in a few cases (peaks of 24 to 47 mrad across seeds where the feedforward PD peaks at 6), which cost it the cases where the nominal model is already accurate (Section 2.4, free-motion clip); the ideal-robot labels teach the model correction, not a bound on the correction's rate.
  9. Not attempted. The real robot (Milestone 7, unauthorised), a second robot and task conditioning (Milestone 8), the representation study, and every claim rests on three seeds and sixteen test cases per family.

7. What to revisit when scaling up

The recipe was developed on a laptop with runs of under an hour. The following items were set once, at that budget, and are where more data, iterations and compute should go first. Each is a question with a measurable answer under the existing protocol.

  1. Replication before anything else. Five or more seeds per method, 64 or more test cases per family and per task, and three tuning seeds of the classical set with a larger CMA-ES budget. The paired intervals of the present wins are the evidence to read; several contact-family wins are large, the task win and the policy-like miss are not.
  2. The base's budget and reward (item E, not run). 250 iterations of 24 windows were enough for the base to reach 6.9 mrad; the kernel half-width (20 mrad) and the torque-rate weight (0.003) were chosen at that budget and do not distinguish 5 from 7 mrad. With more iterations and batch, re-tune both and test whether the base alone closes the free-motion gap, which would make the ideal-robot teacher unnecessary.
  3. Distillation budget. Twelve iterations of 48 episodes with a 300 thousand-row cap, four epochs per iteration, a teacher-driving schedule of one iteration and a free-space weight of 3, all set once. Scale the rows with the data, lengthen the schedule, and add a smoothness term to the free-space labels (or the oracle's labels on the policy-like family) for the one miss.
  4. The training distribution. Instances and payloads are sampled inside the evaluation ranges by design; compliant surfaces, the task sequence and the held-out workspace are not trained on. With more compute, widen the surface geometries, speeds and payloads of training while keeping at least one group held out per axis so the generalisation claims survive.
  5. Validation. Two to four free-space and eight contact cases per family select checkpoints; every checkpoint is kept. With compute, validate on more cases and report the selection rule with the alternative checkpoint, since selection changed what two methods looked like (Section 5, item 5).
  6. The learned model as the medium. Learned-model gradients matched the known model (M4) and were stable through contact at a third of the cost (D3); on a cluster the finite-difference gradients, whose cost grows with the state dimension, are the expensive part of stage 1. The learned model is also the hardware path of the ideal-robot label (a learned plant model in place of the sampled plant's inverse dynamics).
  7. The teacher. Sweep the oracle's stiffness, floor and blend against the band and the cap, and settle the plant's light-contact detachment (a finite contact patch or a different pad model) before the sliding families are used as evidence.
  8. Architecture, untouched since ADR 0005 and 0007. History length and stride, width 128 and two layers, the 10 ms decision period, the output bounds, an output low-pass and Fourier features. None was varied on plant v3; a representation study is cheap next to training and was deferred twice.
  9. Beyond the protocol. More tasks and task conditioning (Milestone 8), a second robot with its own model and controller, and the real robot once authorised, where the inputs and outputs of the controller are already those a Panda exposes and the open question is the supervision (item 4 of Section 6).
